How to run due diligence without losing the deal

Due diligence starts once the seller accepts your letter of intent and usually runs for 30 to 90 days of exclusivity. The goal is not to collect paper. It is to confirm three things: the cash flow is real, it will survive the change of owner, and nothing hidden can take it away.

Start with the money

Ask for the financial items first. If the tax returns, the profit-and-loss statements and the bank deposits do not tell the same story, everything else can wait. An accountant who does quality-of-earnings work for small deals will rebuild seller's discretionary earnings from source documents and flag add-backs that do not hold up.

Then test transferability

Many businesses are profitable because of one person, one customer or one lease. Read the lease and key contracts for assignment and change-of-control clauses, talk to key staff once the seller agrees, and map every task the owner does each week.

Bring in professionals at the right time

An attorney should review the purchase agreement, liens and licences. An accountant should handle the financial review and the tax structure of the deal. Lenders will commission their own valuation. Budget for these costs in your financing plan.

Keep a running issues list

Every gap you find is either fixed before closing, priced into the deal, covered by a holdback or seller note, or a reason to walk away. Agree which before the exclusivity period ends.

This checklist is general information, not legal or financial advice. Requirements vary by country, state and industry.
