How to value a small business

A small business is worth what its cash flow can pay back, adjusted for how risky that cash flow is. Here is how buyers, brokers and lenders turn that idea into a number.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 9 minutes to read.

Value is a range, not a number

Two informed buyers can value the same business differently because they see different risks and different upside. A good valuation gives you a defensible range and explains what would move the business to the top or bottom of it. That is far more useful than one precise-looking figure.

SDE or EBITDA?

Seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) is the standard measure for owner-operated businesses, generally those with less than about $1 million to $2 million of cash flow. It counts everything one full-time owner takes out: profit plus the owner's salary and perks.

EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization) is used for larger businesses with a management team in place. It deducts a market salary for every manager, including the one replacing the owner. Moving from SDE to EBITDA usually lowers the earnings figure but raises the multiple, because larger, management-run businesses are less risky.

How to calculate SDE

Start with pre-tax net profit from the tax return or financial statements and add back:

Add-backExampleSurvives diligence if
Owner salary and payroll taxes$70,000Shown on payroll records
Owner perksCar, phone, health insurance: $9,000Receipts show they are personal and will stop
Interest$6,000Debt will be paid off at closing
Depreciation and amortization$12,000Equipment replacement is budgeted separately
One-time costsLegal dispute: $5,000Documented and genuinely non-recurring

Be sceptical of add-backs that are not on paper. Lenders reject unverifiable add-backs, and a buyer who pays for them is paying for cash flow that may not exist.

What multiples the market is paying

For small businesses, value is most often expressed as a multiple of SDE. In its Q3 2026 Insight Report, BizBuySell reported an average cash-flow multiple of 2.30x for businesses that sold for under $1 million and 3.73x for those that sold for more, with a median sale price of $350,000. Earlier in 2026 the report put the market-wide average at about 2.7x.

Those are averages across industries and quality levels. Restaurants and small retail tend to trade lower; businesses with contracted, recurring revenue, a manager in place or specialist licences tend to trade higher.

The three valuation approaches

Market approach

Value comes from what similar businesses have sold for, expressed as multiples of SDE, EBITDA or revenue. It is the approach most buyers and brokers use for Main Street deals because it reflects what buyers actually pay.

Income approach

Value is the present value of future cash flows, either by capitalising one normalised year or by discounting several projected years. It suits businesses with predictable growth and is common in formal appraisals and lender valuations. Small-business discount rates are high, often well above 20%, which reflects the risk of relying on one location or a small team.

Asset approach

Value is the fair market value of the assets minus liabilities. It sets a floor for asset-heavy businesses and is the main method when a business earns little more than a fair return on its equipment, property and inventory.

What moves the multiple

  • Owner dependence. If customers, quoting and problem-solving all depend on the owner, buyers discount heavily.
  • Customer concentration. A single customer above roughly 15–20% of revenue is a risk to price in.
  • Recurring revenue. Contracts, memberships and subscriptions earn premiums.
  • Trend. Buyers price the last three years, not the best one.
  • Record quality. Books that reconcile to tax returns and bank deposits keep the multiple intact.
  • Transferability. Leases, licences and contracts that pass smoothly to a new owner.

A worked example

A commercial cleaning company reports $110,000 of pre-tax profit. The owner pays herself $65,000, runs $6,000 of personal vehicle costs through the business, has $8,000 of depreciation and paid a one-off $4,000 legal bill. SDE is $193,000.

The company has steady contracts and no client above 12% of sales, but the owner still does most of the selling. A buyer might start at about 2.4x for its size, add a little for contracted revenue and subtract for owner dependence, landing near 2.3x to 2.6x. That gives a range of roughly $445,000 to $500,000, before inventory or real estate. Try your own numbers in the valuation calculator.

Asking price versus sale price

Asking prices are opening positions. Sellers often accept somewhat less than they ask, and terms such as a seller note, an earn-out or a long transition can bridge the remaining gap. Negotiate the whole package, not just the headline number.

When to get a professional valuation

Get a formal valuation when you are financing with an SBA or bank loan (the lender will order one anyway), dealing with partners, estates or divorce, or when tax structure matters. For early planning, a calculator plus an experienced broker's opinion is usually enough.

This guide is general information, not a formal appraisal or financial advice.
