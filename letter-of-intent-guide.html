The letter of intent: your deal on two pages

A letter of intent (LOI) turns a conversation into a framework. Most of it is non-binding, yet it sets the terms everyone negotiates from for the rest of the deal. Get it right and the purchase agreement writes itself.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 7 minutes to read.

Binding and non-binding parts

The commercial terms in an LOI, such as price and structure, are usually stated to be non-binding until a definitive purchase agreement is signed. A few clauses are normally binding from signature: exclusivity (the seller stops talking to other buyers for a period), confidentiality, and sometimes costs and governing law. Read those clauses as carefully as you would a contract, because they are one.

What a strong LOI covers

Price and structure

State the total price and whether you are buying assets or the company's shares or membership interests. Say which assets are included, such as equipment, inventory, intellectual property, customer lists and domain names, and which are excluded, such as cash and personal vehicles.

How the price is paid

Break the price into cash at closing, bank or SBA financing, a seller note (amount, rate, term, security and any standby period) and any earn-out (metric, period and calculation). Vague payment terms are the most common source of later disputes.

Working capital and inventory

Agree how much working capital stays in the business at closing, or that the seller keeps receivables and pays payables. If inventory is extra, say it will be counted near closing and valued at cost, with a cap.

Due diligence and exclusivity

Set the length of the diligence period, often 30 to 90 days, the information the seller will provide, and access to premises, staff and key customers at agreed times.

Conditions

List what must happen before you are obliged to close: satisfactory diligence, financing approval on stated terms, landlord consent to assign the lease, transfer of licences, and any franchisor approval.

Transition

Specify how long the seller will stay, how many hours a week, and whether that time is paid. A clear transition plan also matters to lenders.

Non-compete and non-solicit

Describe the area, length and scope of the seller's promise not to compete or poach staff and customers. What is enforceable varies by jurisdiction.

Employees, timing and costs

Note which employees you expect to offer jobs to, the target closing date, and that each side pays its own advisers.

An LOI outline

  1. Parties and the business being acquired
  2. Purchase price and allocation approach
  3. Assets included and excluded, or equity being acquired
  4. Payment: cash, financing, seller note, earn-out
  5. Working capital and inventory
  6. Due diligence scope and period
  7. Conditions to closing
  8. Seller transition and training
  9. Non-compete and non-solicit
  10. Employees and key contracts
  11. Exclusivity (binding)
  12. Confidentiality (binding)
  13. Target closing date and expiry of the offer
  14. Non-binding nature of the remaining terms

Use this outline to prepare for a conversation with your attorney, not as a substitute for one.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving terms "to be agreed". Every open point becomes leverage for the other side later.
  • Exclusivity that is too short for your lender's timeline, or too long for the seller to accept.
  • No financing contingency, leaving you exposed if the lender's valuation comes in low.
  • Ignoring the lease. Without landlord consent, many businesses cannot transfer.
  • Overpromising on price to win exclusivity, then trying to cut it after diligence. Sellers remember.
