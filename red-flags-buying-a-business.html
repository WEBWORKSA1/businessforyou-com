15 red flags when buying a business

None of these automatically kills a deal. Each one demands an explanation, a lower price, better terms or protection in the purchase agreement. Several together usually mean walk away.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 7 minutes to read.

Financial red flags

1. The books do not reconcile

Tax returns, profit-and-loss statements and bank deposits should tell the same story. When they do not, the seller's preferred version is rarely the right one.

2. Add-backs you cannot verify

"The owner's family members are on payroll but don't really work" and "lots of personal expenses run through the business" may be true, but if they cannot be documented, lenders will not count them and neither should you.

3. "There's a lot of cash you won't see on the books"

Unreported cash cannot be verified, financed or legally relied on. Value the business on what is reported.

4. A recent decline, or a spike just before the sale

Ask for 36 months of monthly revenue. A downward trend explains the timing of many sales; a sudden spike may be engineered.

5. Slow-paying customers and growing payables

Ageing receivables and stretched supplier payments can signal a cash squeeze the profit figures hide.

6. Deferred maintenance and capital spending

If equipment, vehicles or premises are worn out, profit has been flattered by not replacing them. Budget the catch-up.

Customer and market red flags

7. One customer pays a large share of the bills

A customer above roughly 15–20% of revenue can change the business overnight. Read the contract, and consider an earn-out tied to retention.

8. Customers loyal to the owner, not the business

If clients call the owner's personal phone, expect some to leave with the owner unless the transition is long and well planned.

9. A new competitor or a changing market

Check for new entrants, technology shifts and regulatory changes the seller may not mention.

Legal and operational red flags

10. A short lease or no landlord consent

Without a secure location, you could buy a business that has to move or close. Get consent to assignment and an adequate term before closing.

11. Licences or contracts that will not transfer

Some permits, carrier appointments, franchise rights and supplier agreements end when ownership changes. Confirm each one in writing.

12. Pending legal, tax or regulatory issues

Search for liens, lawsuits and tax arrears. Structure the purchase, usually as an asset purchase, and the agreement to leave historic liabilities with the seller.

13. Staff about to leave, or misclassified workers

Key employees resigning during the sale and contractors who should legally be employees are both costly surprises.

Behavioural red flags

14. A seller who resists diligence

Delays, partial documents and pressure to "trust me" are information. A good business survives a careful buyer.

15. Urgency without a reason

"Another buyer is about to sign" may be true. It is also the oldest pressure tactic. Keep to your process and timetable.

What to do when you find one

  • Investigate until you understand the cause and the size of the risk.
  • Price it by adjusting the multiple or the earnings you pay for.
  • Restructure with a seller note, earn-out, holdback or escrow.
  • Protect yourself with representations, warranties and indemnities in the purchase agreement.
  • Walk away when the risk cannot be measured or covered.
