How to buy an online business

Websites, online stores, software products and newsletters can be bought from anywhere and often run on a few hours a week. They can also be dressed up with screenshots and borrowed traffic. Here is how to buy one with your eyes open.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 8 minutes to read.

Where deals are found

Online businesses change hands on open marketplaces, through specialist brokers who vet and package listings, through founder communities, and by direct approach to site owners. Marketplaces offer volume; brokers typically offer more vetting and higher prices. Whichever route you use, do your own verification. Names of third-party marketplaces belong to their owners and are mentioned here only for orientation.

How online businesses are valued

Most small online businesses are priced as a multiple of the last twelve months of net profit, with the owner's time treated much like SDE in a Main Street business. Software businesses with strong retention are often priced on annual recurring revenue instead. The multiple rises with:

  • Age and stability of revenue over at least 12 to 24 months.
  • Diversified traffic and revenue sources.
  • Low owner time and documented processes.
  • Recurring revenue and low churn.
  • Clean, transferable assets: domain, code, content, supplier agreements.

Diligence checks that catch fake numbers

Verify revenue at the source

Ask for read-only or screen-shared access to payment processors, ad networks, affiliate dashboards and marketplace seller accounts. Compare month-by-month figures with bank deposits. Screenshots and spreadsheets are not evidence.

Verify traffic

Review analytics live. Look for sudden spikes, referral spam, traffic bought shortly before the sale, and how much comes from one search engine, social platform or marketplace.

Check costs that might be hidden

Writers, developers, virtual assistants, software subscriptions and ad spend are easy to leave out. Ask for every recurring expense and check the bank statements.

Check account health and policies

For ecommerce, review marketplace account health, returns and supplier terms. For content sites, check for manual search penalties and thin or copied content. For apps, confirm store standing and data-privacy compliance.

Review the technology

For software, have a developer review the code, hosting, third-party dependencies and documentation. Ask who wrote what, and whether contractors have assigned their rights.

Transferring the business safely

  • Use a reputable escrow service: funds are released only when you confirm the assets have transferred.
  • List every asset in the purchase agreement: domains, hosting, code repositories, social accounts, email lists, supplier contacts, content and trademarks.
  • Change all passwords and two-factor settings on day one.
  • Agree a support period during which the seller answers questions and helps with migration.
  • Include a non-compete covering the niche and the seller's other sites.

Red flags specific to online businesses

  • Revenue that jumps in the months before listing.
  • Most traffic from one source, or from paid traffic not disclosed as such.
  • A seller who will only share screenshots.
  • Earnings from a programme whose terms forbid transfer.
  • Content or images that may infringe someone else's copyright.

General information only. Platform terms and tax treatment vary; take professional advice for larger purchases.
