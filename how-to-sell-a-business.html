How to sell your business

Most owners sell a business once. The buyers across the table, and their lenders, do it constantly. This guide evens the odds: how to price, prepare, market and close a sale without giving away value.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 9 minutes to read.

Decide why, and when

Buyers will ask why you are selling, and your answer shapes their offer. Retirement, a new venture or relocation are understood. "Business is slowing" is not a reason buyers pay for. If the business is in a down year, it may pay to wait for a recovery or fix the cause first. The best time to sell is usually after two or three steady or growing years, not after one spike.

Know your number

Get a valuation range before you talk to any buyer, and certainly before you answer an unsolicited offer. Rebuild your seller's discretionary earnings with documented add-backs and compare the result with current market multiples. Start with the valuation calculator, then ask an adviser to pressure-test it.

Prepare before you go to market

The months before a sale are the cheapest time to raise your price. Focus on what buyers and lenders verify:

  • Books that reconcile to tax returns and bank deposits, with monthly figures for three years.
  • Written processes for sales, operations and billing.
  • A team that can run the business without you for a few weeks.
  • A secure lease or renewal option and landlord willingness to assign it.
  • Contracts that can transfer to a new owner.

Our preparation plan sets this out month by month.

Broker or sell it yourself?

A business broker values your business, markets it confidentially, screens buyers and manages the deal through closing in return for a success fee. Selling yourself saves that fee but you take on the work, the buyer screening and the confidentiality risk. Owners selling to a known buyer, such as an employee, competitor or family member, often do well with just an attorney and accountant. Read working with a business broker for how to choose one.

Market it confidentially

Word that a business is for sale can unsettle staff, customers and suppliers. Professional sales follow a staged release of information:

  1. Blind teaser: industry, region, revenue band, cash flow and asking price, without your name.
  2. Non-disclosure agreement signed by each interested buyer.
  3. Confidential information memorandum with history, operations, staff summary and financials.
  4. Meetings and site visits outside business hours for serious, qualified buyers.

Qualify buyers early

Before you share detailed financials, ask each buyer for proof of funds, their financing plan and relevant experience. A buyer who cannot secure financing wastes months. Ask whether they have spoken to a lender and how much cash they will put in.

Negotiate the whole deal, not just the price

Offers usually arrive as a letter of intent. Compare them on more than the headline number:

  • Cash at closing versus deferred payments.
  • Seller financing: amount, interest, term and security. See seller financing explained.
  • Earn-outs tied to future results you may not control.
  • Working capital and inventory left in the business at closing.
  • Your transition: how long you stay, paid or unpaid.
  • Non-compete scope and length.
  • Exclusivity period and what happens if the buyer's financing fails.

Get through due diligence

Expect the buyer, their accountant and their lender to ask for everything on our due diligence checklist. Assemble it in a shared folder before you list. Answer quickly and consistently; delays and surprises are the most common reasons deals fall apart late.

Close, hand over and plan for tax

Your attorney and the buyer's attorney negotiate the purchase agreement, which sets out representations, warranties, indemnities and holdbacks. How the price is allocated between goodwill, equipment, inventory and a non-compete affects the tax you pay, so involve your accountant before you sign the letter of intent, not after.

After closing, a well-planned handover protects any seller note or earn-out you are owed. Introduce the buyer to key customers and suppliers, train the team on the change, and stay reachable for the period you agreed.
