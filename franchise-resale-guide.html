Buying a franchise resale

A resale is an operating franchise unit sold by its current owner. You get customers, staff and a track record from day one, and you inherit the franchise agreement with all its obligations. Here is what to check.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 7 minutes to read.

Why buy a resale?

A new franchise unit asks you to trust the franchisor's projections and survive a ramp-up period. A resale lets you judge the unit on its own tax returns. Lenders like that too: financing an existing unit with proven cash flow is usually easier than financing a start-up unit.

How resales are priced

Like other small businesses, resales are usually priced as a multiple of the unit's owner cash flow, plus inventory. Brand strength matters, but so do the unit's location, lease, remaining franchise term and condition. A resale priced close to the cost of opening a new unit needs strong cash flow to justify it.

The franchisor's role

Almost every franchise agreement requires the franchisor to approve a transfer. Expect:

  • An application, background and credit checks and an interview.
  • A transfer fee, payable by the seller or buyer depending on the agreement and negotiation.
  • Required training before or soon after closing.
  • Signing the franchisor's current form of agreement, which may have different fees or a different term from the seller's.
  • Sometimes a right of first refusal, letting the franchisor buy the unit on your terms.

Speak to the franchisor's development or transfer team early, before you spend money on diligence.

Checks specific to resales

Remaining term and renewal

Find out how many years remain, whether you sign a fresh term, and what renewal costs. A unit with a short remaining term and an expensive renewal is worth less.

Remodel and upgrade obligations

Many systems require periodic refurbishment. If one is due soon, price it in or ask the seller to complete it before closing.

Compliance and defaults

Ask the franchisor whether the unit is in good standing, has open audit findings or owes royalties. A unit in default may not be transferable until the default is cured.

Territory

Confirm the territory you will have, and whether the franchisor plans new units nearby.

System health

In the United States, Item 20 of the Franchise Disclosure Document shows openings, closures and transfers across the system. A high transfer or closure rate deserves questions. Call current and former franchisees listed there.

Financing a resale

Resales are financed much like other acquisitions: buyer cash, bank or government-backed loans and sometimes a seller note. Some franchisors maintain relationships with lenders who know the brand. Run the deal through the loan calculator using the unit's actual cash flow and the royalties you will pay.

Steps in order

  1. Screen the unit's numbers and ask why the owner is selling.
  2. Contact the franchisor to confirm transferability and approval steps.
  3. Sign an LOI with conditions for franchisor approval and landlord consent.
  4. Complete diligence on the unit and on the franchise system.
  5. Secure financing and complete franchisor training.
  6. Sign the transfer documents, purchase agreement and new franchise agreement, then close.

BusinessForYou.com is not affiliated with any franchisor. Have a franchise attorney review the transfer documents and agreement.
