Working with a business broker

A good broker can be worth far more than the fee. A poor one can cost you a year and a buyer. Here is what brokers do, how they are paid, and how to pick the right one, from both sides of the table.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 7 minutes to read.

What a business broker does

  • Values the business and recommends an asking price.
  • Prepares a blind teaser and a confidential information memorandum.
  • Markets the business to its buyer network and on listing sites without revealing the name.
  • Screens buyers for funds, financing and experience, and collects NDAs.
  • Runs meetings, negotiates offers and keeps diligence, lenders and attorneys moving to closing.

Brokers usually represent the seller. Buyers working with a seller's broker should remember whose interests the broker is paid to serve, even when the broker is helpful.

How brokers are paid

Most Main Street brokers charge a success fee when the sale closes. For smaller deals it is commonly around 10% of the price, often with a minimum fee; for larger deals the percentage typically falls, sometimes on a sliding scale. Some brokers also charge an upfront engagement or valuation fee. Everything is negotiable, and the right fee depends on the work and the buyer pool.

How to choose a broker

  • Relevant deals. Ask how many businesses of your size and industry they sold in the last two years, and at what share of asking price.
  • Buyer reach. Ask where they will market the business and how many active buyers they have.
  • Credentials and associations. Professional designations such as the IBBA's Certified Business Intermediary show training and a code of ethics. Some states require a licence when real estate is involved.
  • Valuation honesty. Be wary of the broker who promises the highest price to win the listing.
  • References. Speak to two or three past sellers.
  • Capacity. Ask how many listings they are handling now, and who will work on yours day to day.

Read the listing agreement closely

ClauseWhat to check
TermLength of the engagement and how you can end it.
ExclusivityWhether you owe a fee if you find the buyer yourself.
Tail periodHow long after the agreement ends a fee is still owed for buyers the broker introduced.
Fee basisWhether the fee applies to the total price, including seller notes, earn-outs, real estate and inventory.
ExpensesMarketing or valuation charges, and whether they are credited against the success fee.

When selling without a broker makes sense

If you already know the buyer, such as a manager, partner, competitor or family member, an experienced attorney and accountant may be all you need. Very small businesses can be hard for brokers to justify. Owners comfortable with marketing, screening and negotiating can list directly, including on BusinessForYou.com, and still bring in advisers for valuation and closing.

For buyers: working with brokers

  • Register with brokers in your target area and industry, and tell them your criteria and financing plan.
  • Respond quickly and professionally. Brokers send their best deals to buyers who close.
  • Do your own valuation and diligence, whatever the broker's package says.

Fees and licensing rules vary by country and state. This guide is general information.
