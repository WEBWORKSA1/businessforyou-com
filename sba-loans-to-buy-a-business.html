SBA loans to buy a business

In the United States, the SBA 7(a) program finances a large share of small-business acquisitions. It lets buyers borrow against a business's cash flow with less cash down than a typical bank loan. Here is how it works and how to give your application its best chance.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 8 minutes to read.

What a 7(a) loan is

The U.S. Small Business Administration does not usually lend the money itself. Approved banks and lenders make the loan and the SBA guarantees part of it, which lets lenders finance deals they would otherwise decline, including purchases where most of the price is goodwill rather than hard assets.

According to the SBA, 7(a) loans can go up to $5 million, and changes of ownership, complete or partial, are an eligible use of funds. Acquisition loans for a business without significant real estate are commonly repaid over about ten years; when real estate makes up a large part of the deal, terms can be longer.

Lenders also finance working capital and closing costs within the loan when the cash flow supports it. Try different splits in the loan calculator.

What lenders check

Cash flow coverage

The business must cover its debt payments after paying a reasonable salary to the person running it. Many lenders look for a debt service coverage ratio of at least about 1.25x on historical, verifiable numbers. Projections help, but they rarely rescue a deal whose history does not support the loan.

You

Expect questions about credit history, personal finances and liquidity, and, above all, relevant management or industry experience. A buyer with no experience in the industry may need a strong transition plan, a key manager staying on, or a partner who brings that experience.

The business

Lenders want two to three years of tax returns that agree with the financial statements, a stable or growing trend, and no single point of failure that would sink repayment. The lender will commission an independent business valuation, and the price you agree must be supported by it.

Guarantees and collateral

Owners of 20% or more of the borrowing business generally must personally guarantee an SBA loan. Lenders take security over business assets and may take a lien on personal real estate when the loan is not fully secured.

Documents to prepare

  • Business tax returns for the last three years and year-to-date financial statements.
  • The signed letter of intent or purchase agreement.
  • Your personal tax returns, personal financial statement and résumé.
  • Proof of the cash you will inject, and its source.
  • A short business plan with a transition plan and, ideally, monthly projections.
  • Lease, key contracts, equipment list and any franchise documents.

Timeline

From a complete application, many acquisition loans take several weeks to a few months to close, depending on the lender, the valuation and third-party reports. Lenders with dedicated acquisition teams and delegated SBA authority tend to move fastest. Build that time into your letter of intent's financing contingency and exclusivity period.

Why applications fail

  • Add-backs the lender cannot verify, which push coverage below the minimum.
  • A price above what the independent valuation supports.
  • Declining revenue in the most recent year.
  • Insufficient or undocumented buyer cash.
  • Little relevant experience and no plan to replace the seller's know-how.
  • Tax returns that disagree with internal statements.

Outside the United States

Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, India and other countries run their own government-backed small-business lending schemes, with different rules on goodwill, guarantees and eligible uses. Ask local banks which programmes they use for acquisitions; seller financing is common everywhere and fills many gaps.

Program rules, fees and rates change. Treat this guide as general information and confirm current requirements with an SBA lender or on sba.gov.
