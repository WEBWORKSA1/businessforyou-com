How to buy a business, step by step

Buying a business that already makes money skips the hardest years of starting one. It also brings its own risks. This is the full process, in the order experienced buyers follow it.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 10 minutes to read.

Why buy instead of start?

A business for sale comes with customers, staff, suppliers and a record of cash flow. That record is what makes acquisition financing possible: a lender can underwrite a business that has three years of tax returns in a way it cannot underwrite an idea. The trade-off is price. You pay up front for goodwill, and you inherit whatever problems the seller has not fixed.

Across the United States, BizBuySell counted 2,132 closed small-business sales in the third quarter of 2026 at a median sale price of $350,000. That is the scale most first-time buyers work at.

1. Set your search criteria

Buyers who define criteria before they browse close faster and overpay less. Write down:

  • Industries where your experience gives you an edge, and ones you will not consider.
  • Size as a range of owner cash flow (SDE), not revenue. Cash flow decides what you can borrow and what you can earn.
  • Location and whether you will relocate.
  • Your role: working owner, manager of managers, or semi-absentee investor.
  • Deal-breakers: weekend hours, heavy regulation, a single dominant customer.

2. Line up financing early

Talk to lenders before you find the business. They will tell you how much cash you need, which industries they like and what experience they expect from you. With bank or SBA financing, buyers commonly put in 10% or more of the price in cash, plus closing costs and working capital. Use the loan calculator to turn your cash into a realistic price range.

3. Find deals

Deals come from four places, and serious buyers use all of them:

  • Online marketplaces, including the BusinessForYou.com board, where brokers and owners list publicly.
  • Business brokers, who often show their best listings to registered buyers first. Get on their lists.
  • Direct outreach to owners in your target industry. Many owners nearing retirement have never listed.
  • Your network: accountants, attorneys, bankers and industry suppliers hear about sales before anyone else.

4. Screen listings fast

You will look at dozens of businesses to buy one. Spend five minutes on each listing before you sign anything:

  • Is the price a sensible multiple of cash flow? Most owner-operated businesses sell for about two to four times SDE.
  • Is revenue steady or rising over three years?
  • Why is the owner selling, and does the reason make sense?
  • Does the business depend on one customer, one supplier or the owner personally?

5. Sign an NDA and review the information

For promising listings, sign the seller's non-disclosure agreement to receive the confidential information memorandum and summary financials. Ask early for tax returns. If a seller resists sharing them at this stage, that alone is useful information.

6. Value the business and make an offer

Rebuild seller's discretionary earnings yourself and test every add-back. Apply a multiple that reflects the risks you found, then run the deal through financing: can the cash flow cover the debt after paying you a market salary? Our valuation guide and calculator walk through both. When the numbers work, submit a letter of intent that sets out price, structure, timing and the conditions you need.

7. Do your due diligence

Once the seller signs your letter of intent, you usually get 30 to 90 days of exclusivity to verify everything. Confirm the cash flow against bank records, read every contract and the lease, meet key staff, and check licences, liens and taxes. Use our 61-point checklist and hire an accountant and an attorney who work on small acquisitions.

8. Finalise financing and the purchase agreement

Your lender will order its own valuation and review your diligence. Meanwhile your attorney negotiates the purchase agreement. Most small deals are structured as asset purchases, which let the buyer pick the assets and leave most historic liabilities with the seller. Larger or licence-heavy businesses are sometimes sold as share or equity purchases. The structure changes tax outcomes for both sides, so get advice before you agree.

9. Close and lead the transition

At closing, funds move, ownership documents are signed and the keys change hands. The first 100 days decide whether the business you bought stays the business you paid for. Meet every employee, call the top customers with the seller, keep changes small until you understand why things are done the way they are, and track weekly cash.

How long it takes

StageTypical time
Criteria, financing pre-qualification2–4 weeks
Search and screening1–6 months, sometimes longer
Offer and letter of intent1–3 weeks
Due diligence and financing approval30–90 days
Purchase agreement and closing2–6 weeks

Your deal team

  • Acquisition lender for financing and an early reality check.
  • Accountant for the financial review and tax structure.
  • Attorney for the letter of intent, purchase agreement and closing.
  • Insurance agent to bind cover from the closing date.
  • Optionally a buy-side adviser or mentor who has bought a business before.
