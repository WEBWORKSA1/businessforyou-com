Seller financing explained

When the seller lends part of the purchase price, everyone's incentives line up: the buyer needs less cash, the lender sees a seller who believes in the business, and the seller often gets a better price. Here is how it works.

BusinessForYou.com Editorial Team. Updated November 2026. About 7 minutes to read.

What seller financing is

In a seller-financed deal, the buyer pays part of the price at closing and owes the rest to the seller under a promissory note, repaid with interest over an agreed term. The note is usually secured by the business assets and often by a personal guarantee from the buyer.

Seller notes appear in a large share of small-business deals, either alone or alongside a bank or SBA loan. They are common in every market, including countries where government-backed acquisition lending is limited.

Why sellers agree to it

  • More buyers. Financing widens the pool to capable operators with less cash.
  • Better price. Buyers will usually pay more when terms are flexible.
  • Interest income. The seller earns interest on the deferred amount.
  • Signal of confidence. Lenders and buyers read a seller note as a vote of confidence in the business.
  • Possible tax timing benefits. In some jurisdictions instalment sales spread the tax on the gain. Ask an accountant.

Typical terms

Every deal is negotiated, but these ranges are common starting points in Main Street transactions:

TermCommon range
Size of noteAbout 10%–30% of the price
Repayment term3–7 years
Interest rateOften a few points below bank rates, sometimes with interest-only months
SecurityLien on business assets, personal guarantee, sometimes a pledge of shares
RankingSubordinated to any bank or SBA loan

Seller notes alongside an SBA or bank loan

When a bank or SBA lender is involved, the seller note sits behind the bank loan. Lenders may require it to be on "standby", meaning reduced or no payments for a period or for the full loan term, especially if the note is counted toward the buyer's equity. These rules change and differ by lender, so agree the standby terms early and get them in the letter of intent.

A payment example

A buyer agrees to pay $500,000. The seller finances 15%, or $75,000, at 6% over five years. The monthly payment on the note is about $1,450, roughly $17,400 a year. Add that to the bank payments in the loan calculator to check the business can still cover all its debt after a market salary.

Seller note or earn-out?

A seller note is a fixed debt: the buyer owes it regardless of results. An earn-out is contingent: part of the price is paid only if the business hits agreed targets, such as revenue or gross profit, after closing. Earn-outs help bridge disagreements about the future, but they invite disputes over how results are measured. Define the metric, the accounting rules and the seller's access to records precisely.

Negotiation tips

For buyers

  • Ask for seller financing in your first offer; it is a normal request.
  • Trade a slightly higher price for a larger note or longer term if it improves your coverage.
  • Seek a right of offset so you can deduct proven indemnity claims from note payments.

For sellers

  • Check the buyer's experience and credit as carefully as a bank would.
  • Take security and a personal guarantee, and require financial reporting while the note is outstanding.
  • Keep the note to an amount you could afford to lose; it ranks behind the bank.

General information only. Have an attorney draft the note, security agreement and any subordination agreement.
